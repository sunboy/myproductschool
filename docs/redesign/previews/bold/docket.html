Airbnb Experiences lists a host-run cooking class in Lisbon at three price points depending on where a guest discovers it: direct search, an Airbnb Plus recommendation, or a co-host referral. Hosts report the referral channel converts at half the rate of direct search, despite identical listings and identical review scores.1 The product team wants a fix shipped before the summer surge in three weeks.

"The complaint on file names the symptom, not the disease. Conversion is down on one channel. That is an observation, not a frame."

Three parties hold a stake in the outcome, and their incentives do not point the same direction. The guest wants confidence that the price they see is the price they pay. The host wants every channel to carry equal weight so no single referral source can starve their calendar. The referral co-host wants their placement to convert, because their commission depends on it.2 A frame that only serves the guest will read as fair to guests and punitive to hosts.

The reasoning move under review at this exhibit: name the friction before naming the fix. Three prior attempts on this docket named a fix (lower the referral price, add a trust badge, cap referral volume) before establishing what is actually breaking trust between the referred guest and the checkout screen.3

The standing question before the court: is this a pricing-transparency problem, a channel-incentive problem, or a discovery problem in disguise. Your brief must resolve it, cite at least one precedent from the drawer at right, and land on a single problem statement the rest of the case can build on.
