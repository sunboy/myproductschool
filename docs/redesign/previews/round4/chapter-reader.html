Entities vs. Attributes: The Decision That Haunts You Later

Most modeling mistakes aren't about normalization. They're about misidentifying what an entity actually is.

Most modeling mistakes aren't about normalization. They're about misidentifying what an entity actually is, and that decision propagates through every index, migration, and join you write for the next five years.

Here's the test that actually works: does it have its own lifecycle? An entity gets created, updated, and deleted independently of the things around it. An attribute doesn't exist without its parent. A customer has an address, but the address isn't an entity unless you need to track when it changed, who verified it, or whether it's shared across multiple customers. The moment any of those questions matter, the address stops being a field and starts being a table.

This is where most schemas go wrong early. A team models "status" as a column because it feels like a property of the order. Then someone asks for a status history, an audit trail, or a way to see who changed it and why. Now status needs its own identity, its own timestamps, its own foreign key back to the order. What should have been a table from day one gets bolted on as a migration, and every query that used to read one row now has to join, sort, and filter to find the current value.

The cost isn't visible on day one. It shows up at the moment you least want it: the migration that touches every row in a hot table, the index that has to be rebuilt while traffic keeps flowing, the join that turns a 10ms query into something your monitoring flags at 2am. Getting the entity boundary right up front is cheaper than any optimization you'll apply later.

Figure 1.1 · Entity vs. attribute
Name and email stay attributes on Customer. Address only earns its own box once you need to track when it changed or who verified it.
Rule of thumb: if it has its own lifecycle, it's an entity. If it only ever exists as part of something else, and never needs to be queried, audited, or shared on its own, it's an attribute.

Apply that test before you touch normalization rules. Normalization tells you how to arrange entities once you already know what they are. It has nothing to say about the harder question of where one entity ends and another begins, and that question is the one that decides whether your schema still makes sense at ten times the data volume.
